1 – Choose the course after the TNA, not before

About 30 seconds. Say: A training needs analysis looks at the gap between the performance that is needed and the performance we actually get. Training is one possible answer to that gap. As the meme says, find the gap first, before you choose the course. I will use one case throughout: final year students doing their final year project, which for most of them is their first large solo project.

Reasons for a TNA

  • Organisation: goals, quality and legal duties
  • Individual: the role, confidence and career
  • A good TNA joins the two6

One question

What is the gap, and who has it?

About 30 seconds. Say: Reasons come at two levels. The organisation asks what it needs to meet its goals and its legal duties. The individual asks what they need to do the job well and to grow. In my case, the department wants projects finished on time and to standard, and each student wants to pass and to build a portfolio.

Organisational reasons

  • Performance: projects finished on time and to standard6
  • Compliance: GDPR when students collect user data, and the AI policy1
  • New technology: AI tools change how software is written
Case: lectures teach the subject, not how to run a project. Past students lost work, took on too much and left the report to the end.
About 90 seconds. Anonymised: no institution, staff or student names. Say: Three organisational reasons. First, performance: the department needs projects that are finished on time and meet the standard. Second, compliance: many projects collect personal data from test users, so students must follow GDPR, and they must follow the college's AI policy. Third, new technology: AI tools are changing how software is written, and students need to use them responsibly. The case: lectures teach the language and the theory, but nobody teaches the habits a project needs. Over several years, the same problems come back: work lost with no backup, a scope that is far too big, and a report started in the last week. That pattern is the reason for a TNA, rather than another lecture. [Once the survey is in, add one real figure here, for example the share of students who have no backup.]

Individual reasons

  • Skills gap: planning, backup and report writing
  • Confidence: the first large solo project
  • Career development: the project is the portfolio
  • Motivation: adults learn what they see a need for3
About 60 seconds. Say: For the individual student, the first reason is a skills gap. They can program, but they have never planned, backed up and written up a project of this size on their own. The second is confidence: a first large solo project is daunting, and a gap in confidence shows up as procrastination. The third is career development: the project and its repository are what they show employers. And motivation: Knowles et al. (2020) argue that adults learn best when they can see why it matters to them, so the TNA has to start from the student's own view of the gap.

2 – Without a TNA, the budget follows the shiny course

About 2 minutes. Be critical: give the counter-point. Say: For the organisation, the main benefit is that training goes where the gaps are, not to the latest course that catches management's eye, as in the meme. Limited resources such as supervisor time are not wasted. It also improves results and leaves a record that shows compliance. For the individual, the benefits are new skills, more confidence, career development and job satisfaction. But a TNA has real limits. It takes staff time to design and run. Self-ratings can be wrong, because people overrate skills they have never had to use. Some will resist and see it as extra work, or as a judgement on them. And the findings go out of date, because each year's group is different. So a TNA is worth it only if it is kept short and acted on.

3 – Each method covers another's weakness

About 2 minutes. Compare, then say when you would use each. Say: A questionnaire reaches many people quickly and cheaply, so it suits a first pass. Adding a skills audit, where students rate their confidence in each skill, shows where the gaps are. But the questions must be tested first, and the answers are shallow. An interview gets to root causes and lets you follow up, but it takes time and needs a skilled interviewer, and experts may only tell you what they want you to hear. Observation shows what people really do, and documents show what is required, but people change their behaviour when they are watched. No single method is enough, so I would combine them, as the brains show for my case: not just asking one supervisor, but an anonymous survey of the students, then short interviews with supervisors to explain what the survey finds, then the marking rubric to check what the project actually requires.

Stages in the TNA process

Stage5Why it matters
1. Identify the purposeOtherwise you collect the wrong data
2. Analyse organisation, task, individualTraining may not be the answer
3. Choose and test the methodsUntested questions give poor data
4. Collect the dataEvidence, not opinion
5. Analyse and prioritiseTime and money are limited
6. Recommend and planFindings become action
7. Evaluate2Did it close the gap?
About 90 seconds. Justify each stage in one sentence. The slide below is optional: skip it if time is short. Say: Each stage protects the next one. If the purpose is unclear, you collect the wrong data. Analysing at three levels, organisation, task and individual, stops you training a person for what is really a process problem. Testing the questionnaire first avoids poor data. Collecting data gives evidence instead of opinion. Analysis takes the most time, and prioritising matters because time and money are limited. Recommending and planning turns findings into action. And evaluation, for example with Kirkpatrick's four levels, shows whether the training closed the gap and feeds the next TNA.

Conclusion

4 – Skip evaluation and nobody knows if it worked

About 30 seconds. Say: A TNA is the foundation for training that is worth the time. Start with the gap, not the course. Use more than one method, because each covers another's weakness. Link what you find to the goals of both the organisation and the individual. And evaluate, or you end up like the dog: training delivered, never evaluated, and everything is fine. For my case, the next step is the survey, and then a training intervention built on what it finds. Thank you. I am happy to take questions.
